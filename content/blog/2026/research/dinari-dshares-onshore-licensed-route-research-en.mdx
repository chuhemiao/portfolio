## TL;DR

**Dinari is the only issuer already operating on the onshore-licensed route.** US jurisdiction, underlying held with a qualified custodian, a direct-security structure, **724 instruments covering the entire S&P 500** (the widest in the category), and the **only issuer in this series open to US persons** — accredited investors only.

It is also small enough to appear on no size table. The top five are Ondo, bStocks, xStocks, Robinhood and Remora. Dinari is not among them.

**My read: before the SEC Innovation Exemption of 2026-09-17, regulation was the ceiling on this route. After it, the binding constraint becomes distribution. Dinari has the structure, the coverage and the US channel; what it lacks is a way to turn those into scale.**

**One thing up front: this is a structural piece, not a market-data one.** Neither research source gives Dinari's on-chain value, token-level volume or book depth — those cells are empty. I mark every gap explicitly rather than filling it with inference.

## The structure: unlike everyone else

Put the first three issuers in this series beside it and the difference is immediate:

| Issuer | Jurisdiction | Wrapper | US persons | Instruments |
|---|---|---|---|---:|
| Ondo Stocks | British Virgin Islands | Issuer claim | Blocked | 100+ |
| bStocks | ADGM | Tracker certificate | Blocked | ~80 |
| xStocks | Jersey | Tracker certificate | Blocked | 100+ |
| **Dinari dShares** | **United States** | **Direct security** | **Accredited** | **724** |

Three dimensions inverted at once: **onshore jurisdiction, a direct security rather than a debt certificate, and US access.**

That "direct security" classification matters. The SEC's Statement on Tokenized Securities (2026-01-30) splits the market into four structures, from "tokenized by the issuer or its agent" down to "the token provides price exposure only". **xStocks and Ondo sit toward the back half — an SPV or issuer credit stands between the token and the security. Dinari's underlying is held by a qualified custodian, which places it further forward.**

"Qualified custodian" is not marketing language. It is a defined concept under US securities law, carrying specific eligibility requirements and client-asset segregation duties. **That is considerably more concrete than a generic "regulated custodian".**

## What 724 instruments means

Coverage across the field: Dinari **724**, Robinhood 190+, Ondo 100+, xStocks 100+ (the September source reported 714 including ETFs), bStocks ~80, Coinbase 10.

**724 covering the full S&P 500** means this is not a selection of popular names — it is a complete index.

But coverage is an easy metric to abuse. The same problem has appeared in both previous pieces: **"contracts deployed" and "instruments with live supply" are different numbers.** Coinbase deployed thirteen contracts with supply in four; xStocks shows 714 and 100+ side by side.

**For Dinari's 724 I cannot separate the two — that is the first data gap.** Until I have a count of instruments with non-zero supply, 724 is a ceiling rather than actual coverage.

## The only US channel, and what it costs

**Accredited-investor access** is Dinari's most distinctive property on this table. Every other issuer blocks US persons outright.

But the accredited threshold in the US is a real barrier: $200,000 of individual income ($300,000 jointly) for two consecutive years, or $1,000,000 in net worth excluding the primary residence.

**That threshold explains the size question.** A product open only to accredited investors has given up the retail market by construction — while bStocks, with a $5 minimum and the Binance funnel, reached $914M in four months. **They are not competing for the same users.**

So it is wrong to say Dinari "lost" to bStocks. **They answer different questions: bStocks asks how to let the most people buy it; Dinari asks how to let the people who buy it actually own it.**

## The regulatory turn: the binding constraint is moving

Two things in 2026 changed this route's outlook.

**SEC Innovation Exemption (2026-09-17)**: qualifying tokenized securities venues may trade tokenized NMS stock through permissioned AMM pools without registering as an exchange or ATS, subject to caps on instruments and volume; market makers are separately relieved of broker registration. It covers both issuer-direct and unaffiliated third-party tokens.

**Nasdaq's same-order-book rule approved**: qualifying tokenized stocks and ETFs may trade in the same order book, at the same execution priority, as the traditional share — provided the two are fungible and carry identical rights. DTCC reports 50+ institutions in its tokenization service, and the DTC received SEC no-action relief in December 2025 for a three-year pilot.

Read together: **"identical rights and fungible" is becoming the entry ticket to US liquidity.** And that is precisely what the offshore route cannot structurally obtain — a tracker certificate or an issuer claim does not, by definition, carry the same rights as the share.

**Dinari's structure sits natively closer to that threshold.** It is its largest source of option value, and the only reason I keep tracking it.

Worth flagging: **the reported five-year term and equal-rights conditions attached to the Innovation Exemption are secondary readings and should be checked against the SEC order itself.** Details at that level decide who gets through the door; they are not something to take second-hand.

## The data I do not have

Stated plainly, these are the questions I cannot answer today:

1. **On-chain value** — empty in both sources' issuer tables, so no comparison against $914M / $847M is possible
2. **Token-level liquidity** — no dShares token appears on any market-cap or volume leaderboard; spread, depth and turnover are all unknown
3. **How many of the 724 carry live supply** — the real denominator of that coverage figure
4. **Redemption terms** — cash or in-kind, minimum ticket, settlement time, fees
5. **Proof of reserve** — whether a public PoR exists, at what cadence, and whether it reconciles against on-chain supply
6. **Voting and dividend handling** — a direct-security structure should preserve both, but I found no explicit statement

**Items 4, 5 and 6 are core grading inputs.** Until they are filled in, Dinari stays at B — not because the structure is weak, but because the verifiable information is insufficient. That is the same reason bStocks stays at B.

## What I will watch

1. **Whether it appears on rwa.xyz or CoinGecko issuer tables** — becoming measurable is itself a signal
2. **How many of the 724 have live supply** — turning coverage from a marketing number into a usable one
3. **Formal disclosure of redemption and proof of reserve** — the only path from B to A
4. **Whether it is on the first TSV approval list** — the dividing line between a structural advantage and an actual channel
5. **Any loosening of the accredited threshold** — any move toward retail resets the size expectation entirely

---

**Bottom line.** Dinari is structurally the most correct issuer in the category: onshore jurisdiction, qualified custody, direct securities, the widest coverage, the only US channel. **But a correct structure is not a working product — it is small enough to appear on no size table, and I cannot even obtain its on-chain value.**

The regulatory environment is moving in its favour. **Until the first TSV approvals are published and its redemption and reserve mechanisms are publicly verifiable, I treat it as a structural option rather than a tool anyone can use today.**
