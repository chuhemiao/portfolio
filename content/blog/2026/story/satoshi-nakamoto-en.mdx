On October 31, 2008, an email arrived on a cryptography mailing list.

The sender was Satoshi Nakamoto. Attached was a white paper titled _Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System_.

Nobody paid attention.

---

## The Beginning: A Halloween White Paper

It was 2008, and the financial crisis was unfolding. Lehman Brothers had just collapsed, and the world was losing confidence in the banking system.

Satoshi chose that moment to publish his white paper. Whether it was coincidence or deliberate timing is unknown.

The paper was only 9 pages long. With concise language and rigorous technical reasoning, it addressed a question cryptographers had debated for twenty years: **how could electronic cash move directly between peers without a trusted third party?**

The central difficulty was double spending: spending the same money twice. The conventional answer was to rely on a bank. Satoshi's answer was to rely on mathematics and game theory.

He designed a mechanism in which transactions were recorded on a public chain, with every node retaining a complete copy. Altering a transaction would require control of more than 51% of the computing power. In theory, this made the cost of fraud much greater than the reward for acting honestly.

---

## The Genesis Block: A Message Written into History

On January 3, 2009, Satoshi mined Bitcoin's first block, known as the genesis block.

He embedded a line of text:

> _The Times 03/Jan/2009 Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks_

It was that day's front-page headline in _The Times_. The British government was preparing another rescue of the banking sector.

Satoshi did not explain what the line meant. But everyone understood what it was saying.

---

## A Mysterious Presence

For the next two years, Satoshi was active on forums and mailing lists, answering technical questions, fixing bugs, and debating design details with early developers.

But nobody had seen him.

Nobody knew whether he was one person or a team. Nobody knew his time zone: his replies were scattered enough to frustrate attempts to infer his location. His English was excellent, though he sometimes used British spellings, such as “colour” instead of “color.”

He never discussed his personal information.

---

## Disappearance

In late 2010, Satoshi began to become less active.

He handed control of the code repository to developer Gavin Andresen and transferred the Bitcoin project's contact email to another developer. Then he sent his final email:

> _I've moved on to other things._

By April 2011, he had disappeared completely.

His Bitcoin wallets held approximately one million bitcoins, which had never moved.

---

## Who Is Satoshi?

Speculation about his identity never stopped after his disappearance.

Some suggested Dorian Nakamoto, a Japanese-American mathematician. Reporters confronted him outside his home in 2014, and he denied everything.

Some suggested cypherpunk pioneer Hal Finney, who received the first Bitcoin transfer in history and died from ALS in 2014.

Some suggested Australian computer scientist Craig Wright, who repeatedly claimed to be Satoshi but never supplied convincing cryptographic proof.

Others suggested that Satoshi was a team rather than a person—perhaps the NSA or an academic institution.

Nobody knows the answer.

---

## What He Left Behind

Bitcoin's price passed $100,000 in 2024.

If the one million coins he mined were still there, they would be worth more than $100 billion. But they had never moved.

Perhaps he died. Perhaps he chose permanent silence. Perhaps he is somewhere watching his creation change the world, saying nothing.

The line he left in the genesis block remains inscribed on the chain:

> _Chancellor on brink of second bailout for banks_

It was a message for everyone, and for the future.

Satoshi understood one thing: **code lasts longer than people, and mathematics is more dependable than politics.**

He created Bitcoin and then disappeared.

Perhaps that itself is the answer.
