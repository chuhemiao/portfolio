An LLM agent's performance is never determined by the model alone. An often underestimated layer also matters: the harness, the system surrounding the model, including system prompts, tools, memory, validation rules, orchestration, and failure recovery. Changing the harness around the same model can more than double performance. That observation is familiar, but it creates an increasingly difficult engineering problem. Models differ in failure modes, tool-use habits, and sensitivity to prompt formats. A harness that works for model A may be suboptimal for model B. New models are now released faster than people can manually tune for them, and building a custom harness for every release is becoming prohibitively expensive.

This paper from Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory proposes a direct response: Self-Harness lets an agent modify its own harness, without a human engineer or a stronger external agent guiding it. This explicitly differs from the earlier Meta-Harness approach, which uses a stronger external agent to optimize the harness of a weaker one and therefore still relies on outside guidance. Self-Harness brings the improvement loop inside the target agent. The same model both performs the evaluated tasks and proposes modifications. The distinction determines where the method applies. For frontier models without a stronger external agent, or when external guidance does not match the target model's failure modes, internal self-improvement is the only viable path.

The mechanism is a three-stage iterative loop. First comes Weakness Mining. The model runs a batch of tasks under its current harness, then clusters failed execution traces using a three-part signature: the verifier's final failure reason, the causal role of the agent's behavior in that failure, and the abstract behavioral mechanism exposed. A crucial design choice is that two failures with the same apparent verifier error, such as timeouts, are not grouped together if their underlying agent behaviors differ, because they need different harness changes. The purpose of clustering is to identify failures that the same harness-level intervention could resolve, rather than merely find semantic similarity.

Second comes Harness Proposal. The same model takes the proposer role and generates candidate modifications from the clustered failure evidence. Two constraints apply: diversity and minimality. Different candidates must target different failure mechanisms or editing surfaces, rather than rephrase one change. Each individual candidate must also remain narrow, changing only what its target mechanism needs while leaving the overall control architecture intact. This addresses a common failure mode: model self-improvement can slide into piling generic instructions onto the system prompt, creating the appearance of optimization without solving any concrete problem.

Third comes Proposal Validation. Every candidate harness is reevaluated on both held-in and held-out task sets. A candidate is accepted into the next harness version only if neither split regresses and at least one improves. This is conservative: it rejects a gain on one split bought by a loss on the other, even at the cost of passing over some candidates. Harness iteration becomes an empirical process with an audit trail. Every version transition has explicit evidence, a defined editing surface, and evaluation results. To me, this auditability matters more than the performance numbers. Self-improvement becomes a sequence of traceable, reproducible, and, when necessary, reversible decisions rather than opaque drift.

The experiments ran three models from different families on Terminal-Bench-2.0: MiniMax M2.5, Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, and GLM-5. They began with a deliberately rudimentary harness containing basic file-reading, writing, and editing tools, shell execution, and a single system instruction. Held-out pass rates rose from 40.5% to 61.9%, 23.8% to 38.1%, and 42.9% to 57.1%, respectively, relative gains of 33% to 60%. The key point is where the gains occurred: on held-out tasks never seen during proposal generation. That suggests the accepted changes addressed reusable execution mechanisms rather than memorized patches for known failures. This is the central test of whether self-improvement generalizes or merely overfits its evaluation set.

The models learned different changes, which itself supports the core hypothesis. MiniMax M2.5 delayed producing required files, handled structured tool output insufficiently carefully, and became trapped in inefficient tool-call loops. Its changes encouraged earlier output creation, more careful treatment of structured tool results, and a cap on total tool messages to force a change of course before loops grew too long. Qwen3.5 acted before checking dependencies, repeated failed commands, and entered endless exploration loops. Its changes added advance dependency checks, interrupted repeated failing commands, and reminded the agent after tool errors to return to producing required files. GLM-5 failed to retain environment setup between shell commands and delayed moving from exploration to implementation and testing. Its changes preserved environment modifications across sessions and added a rule to switch to implementation and validation when continued exploration had produced no required files. Three different execution pathologies received three different interventions. That is stronger evidence of diagnostic intervention than applying one universal remedy and observing higher scores.

The limits of these results also need to be stated honestly, and the paper acknowledges them. First, this is bounded harness editing under a closed benchmark, not open-ended self-improvement. Accepted changes may carry the imprint of Terminal-Bench-2.0, and their optimality on a very different task distribution is uncertain. Second, validation depends on the quality of verifier judgments and execution traces. If a verifier has systematic blind spots, such as misclassifying some failures as passes, the evidence chain built on it also suffers. This is a self-referential risk: the measuring instrument used to judge harness improvement has not itself been independently validated. Third, the acceptance rule supplies only the hard condition that pass counts must not decline. For high-risk agents handling funds or irreversible actions, non-regression on one benchmark is clearly insufficient to authorize a harness change. The paper explicitly notes that higher-risk changes need stronger acceptance gates than non-decreasing pass rates.

In the broader picture, the paper's most interesting contribution is its redefinition of harness engineering as a systematic, auditable, reproducible empirical state-transition process. Harness design has largely relied on engineers' intuition about a particular model, experience that is difficult to retain or transfer and often must be rebuilt for a new model. Self-Harness turns that search itself into a loop with evidence and acceptance rules. As model releases accelerate, some degree of adaptive harnessing may become a necessity, much as automated hyperparameter search already is in model training. Moving from a controlled benchmark to adaptive harnesses in production still requires verifier reliability, acceptance requirements scaled to risk, and generalization across task distributions. This paper does not fully solve those problems. They will determine how far the approach can go, more than the percentage-point improvements currently demonstrated.
