## TL;DR

While organizing accounts and verification methods that I rarely use, I realized I still lacked a cheap, reliable backup number that did not require identity registration. Domestic IoT cards were getting more expensive, and virtual-operator plans could be discontinued abruptly because of policy changes. Meanwhile, some small European operators offered inexpensive eSIMs without asking for identity documents.

After a few days of experimenting, I chose an Estonian number from esim.gg. Once the process worked, I calculated the cost: activation plus the first top-up came to about **RMB 32.5**. Keeping **EUR 3–5** as a long-term balance buffer puts my annual mental budget at about **RMB 50**.

Its role is clear: **a backup number, not my primary identity number. It can receive low-importance verification codes, but should not be tied to payments, exchanges, banks, or accounts I cannot afford to lose.**

## Why I Chose This Setup

My device is a Japanese iPhone with native eSIM support. I did not need a separate card reader or modified firmware. That was one reason I chose this over other options: no hardware tinkering, just a few steps on a website.

esim.gg is an individual reseller project, often called “Turtle Telecom” in Chinese communities. The number is Estonian, the underlying operator is Elisa, and roaming worked in mainland China. Registration did not require identity documents. I signed in with Google and selected a free number range rather than paying extra for a special number.

Activation and my first top-up cost **RMB 32.5** in total. The top-up was **EUR 1**, the minimum amount; the rest covered activation and exchange-rate costs. I paid with Alipay and did not encounter a stuck payment or failed retry.

![esim.gg Estonian number top-up page](/article/esim-gg-estonian-number-recharge.png)

After payment, My Lines provided a QR code. Scanning it with the iPhone's built-in Add eSIM function installed the profile in seconds. Signal appeared almost immediately.

Connecting to that Estonian number for the first time felt tangible. Watching the signal bars appear reminded me of inserting a physical SIM into an old phone for the first time—only there was no plastic card or SIM-eject pin this time.

## Keeping the Number Is the Real Cost

Whether this works long term depends on number-maintenance rules, not activation.

Broadly, esim.gg extends the number's validity automatically when the account balance stays above a threshold and the SIM connects to a base station at least once during the year. There is no monthly manual renewal or periodic keep-alive text.

But the terms changed in February this year: if the SIM has not connected to a base station for an entire year, a maintenance fee is deducted from the balance to extend validity. The amount deducted, and what remains afterward, depends on the balance available at that time.

That is why I do not intend to keep the balance exactly at the minimum.

My **RMB 50 annual estimate** combines the following, rather than considering only the price at activation:

| Item | My approach |
|---|---|
| Activation and first top-up | About RMB 32.5 |
| Minimum top-up | EUR 1 |
| Long-term balance buffer | EUR 3–5 |
| Annual maintenance action | Connect to a base station manually once a year and use a little data |
| Mental budget | About RMB 50 per year |

Keeping EUR 3–5 leaves room if a maintenance fee is charged in some future year. It reduces the risk of losing roaming signal or having the number cancelled because the balance falls below an unclear threshold.

My rule is simple: **once a year, find this eSIM, turn on data, connect to the network, load a webpage to consume a little data, and turn data off again.** A recorded base-station connection is safer than relying on the balance alone.

## The Common Trap with Cheap SIMs

The activation cost is almost negligible. But the main trap is not the upfront price—it is failing to account for long-term upkeep.

I have seen people activate several obscure operators' numbers for a few yuan, only to find the balance exhausted and the number recycled six months later. Accounts tied to those numbers then stopped working. The supposed saving was consumed by account recovery, changing bindings, and verification.

That is why I count the first top-up, exchange-rate cost, and maintenance buffer together, instead of looking only at activation.

More importantly, I do not treat this SIM as the foundation of my identity. I mainly use it for unimportant verification texts or occasional roaming data. It is not linked to identity verification, payment accounts, exchanges, or anything I cannot afford to lose.

## My Conclusion

Turtle Telecom is an individual reseller project with less than two years of operation. The owner himself describes it as a one-man project. Its strengths are low cost, simplicity, and quick responses; its reliability cannot be compared with a large operator's.

These small eSIM services suit a convenient, inexpensive backup number. They do not suit genuinely important dependencies.

For about RMB 50 a year, I consider a foreign backup number that can be maintained long term worthwhile. But it should be one backup path, not the final line of defense for identity security.
