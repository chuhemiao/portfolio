## III. The $80.7B Net Loss: Accounting Losses and Economic Losses Are Different

This is the portion of the prospectus most easily misunderstood.

### Composition of the 2026 H1 Net Loss

Fair-value changes in financing instruments and related items

Approximately $72.4B

Net amount of the remaining items within the net loss

Approximately $8.3B

Total net loss was approximately $80.7B. This is an approximate decomposition, not a complete income statement. The remaining $8.3B cannot be called a cash loss.

Public reporting attributes approximately $72.4B to fair-value adjustments on instruments such as convertible debt, derivative liabilities, and future-equity agreements.

*Source label: Quartz.*

Why can a higher company valuation produce an accounting loss?

Suppose Anthropic issues a financing instrument linked to future equity value. If the company’s valuation rises sharply, the fair value of the equity or corresponding economic benefits it must deliver in the future also rises. If the instrument is classified as a liability under applicable accounting rules, the change in value may be recognized in current-period profit or loss.

This produces an apparently counterintuitive result:

The more valuable the company becomes, the higher the fair value of certain financing liabilities, and the larger its reported current-period loss may be.

But several things must be distinguished:

- Noncash fair-value losses: these do not directly mean that the same amount of cash was paid in the period.
- Actual equity dilution: future conversion terms, conversion share counts, and total post-offering shares may change existing shareholders’ economic interests.
- Operating losses: on a GAAP basis, 2026 H1 still showed a loss of approximately $0.693B.
- Free cash flow: this must be reconstructed separately from operating cash flow, capital expenditure, leases, and financing arrangements. Net income cannot substitute for it.

The correct conclusion is not “Anthropic actually lost only $0.693B.” Its current operating results have improved substantially, but operating cash flow, compute investment, and future equity dilution must still be measured.

Likewise, an adjusted operating margin of positive 10% does not mean shareholders have earned a 10% distributable return. Stock-based compensation, training costs, lease financing, and necessary ongoing capital reinvestment may all ultimately carry economic costs.

## IV. Business Model: Selling Intelligent Computing Capacity to Enterprises

### 4.1 Revenue Comes Mainly from Sources Beyond Ordinary Consumer Subscriptions

Reuters disclosed an approximate breakdown of Anthropic’s 2025 revenue:

The 2025 revenue-source categories below follow the supplied material’s approximate Reuters classification, with amounts and shares as follows.

Approximately $3.8B came from usage-based consumption, and approximately $0.789B from subscriptions: approximately 83% and 17%, respectively, with rounding in the amounts.

*Source label: Investing.com.*

This makes Anthropic closer to an infrastructure platform selling intelligence as a measurable resource than a company relying entirely on ChatGPT-style individual subscriptions.

Its principal revenue relationships include enterprises buying model capabilities through APIs, indirect procurement through cloud marketplaces, Claude Code developer tools, enterprise Claude workflows, and individual subscriptions.

A notable business-model distinction is that traditional SaaS often charges per seat, whereas Anthropic’s core business is highly exposed to token consumption.

A basic economic model for usage-based billing is:

`R = N_workloads × Q_tokens × P_token`

Where:

- `N_workloads` is the number of workloads actually using AI.
- `Q_tokens` is the number of billable tokens per workload.
- `P_token` is average realized revenue per token.

Long-term revenue growth depends on the net effect of these three variables offsetting one another.

For example, if the token unit price falls 80% but actual usage rises 10x, revenue on a comparable basis can still grow to 2x its original level. Conversely, if greater inference efficiency reduces token consumption while prices fall faster than task demand expands, revenue may decline.

The more precise long-term growth driver for AI model companies is therefore expansion in billable workloads, beyond model capabilities or token counts alone.

### 4.2 Claude Code Is One of the Most Important Commercial Validations

In its official February 2026 funding announcement, Anthropic disclosed that Claude Code’s annualized revenue run rate had exceeded $2.5B, with enterprise usage contributing more than half of its revenue. More than 500 customers were also spending at an annualized rate above $1M.

*Source label: Anthropic.*

By September 30, 2026, the company disclosed more than 11,900 customers meeting an annual-revenue threshold of $100,000.

*Source label: Quartz.*

This indicates that enterprise AI commercialization is moving from experimental procurement toward larger-scale production spending.

But validation of willingness to pay must not be equated directly with long-term customer lock-in. Developer tools have significant potential for model substitution. If a competitor offers better cost, latency, accuracy, or code-execution performance, enterprises have an incentive to reconsider the underlying model.

From a moat perspective, Claude Code’s greatest contribution to Anthropic may extend beyond product revenue: it embeds the model in software-development processes, tool use, enterprise permission systems, and daily habits, increasing switching costs.

The real questions are whether enterprise customers keep expanding usage, and whether they remain willing to pay a premium once competing models approach similar performance.

I consider this more worthy of investigation than the headline net loss.

The core mechanism is Take-or-Pay: a minimum contractual amount may remain payable regardless of whether the agreed capacity is fully used.

Such arrangements secure long-term access to computing resources but introduce new operating risks:

- If demand falls short, idle compute capacity may still put pressure on cash flow.
- If model and hardware efficiency improve too rapidly, previously purchased capacity may lose its competitive advantage.
- If new chips rapidly improve price-performance, existing long-term contracts may become uneconomic.
- If inference prices fall faster than compute costs, model-provider margins may face sustained compression.

A September 2026 Reuters investigation said approximately 80% of the roughly $518B in arrangements disclosed at that time involved noncancellable payment obligations or payments not fully adjusted to actual usage. This cannot be extrapolated directly to all contracts after the October update.

*Source label: Reuters.*

### 5.4 A High-Risk Investment or Rational Advance Capacity Procurement?

Anthropic’s decision can be understood as a combination of a real option on long-term compute capacity and fixed purchase commitments.

When compute remains scarce for a long time, locking in resources early protects revenue opportunities. Refusing or delaying customer requests also carries an opportunity cost.

When model efficiency improves rapidly, demand growth slows, or supply becomes excessive, fixed commitments can become a burden.

The key variables determining a contract’s economic value are:

`V_capacity = PV(realizable revenue contribution) − PV(unavoidable capacity costs)`

Revenue contribution here must be net of inference, service delivery, and other incremental costs.

A one-way bullish view of AI does not resolve this question. Even if AI usage keeps rising, earlier procurement decisions can still damage shareholder returns if service prices decline faster or new hardware exceeds the efficiency implicit in old contracts.

### 5.5 Circular Dependencies in AI Capital Expenditure

The supplied material does not include an industry capital-flow diagram. The discussion below describes only investment, supply, and distribution relationships. It does not imply financial misconduct or that financing funds pay for every transaction.

This explains why Anthropic’s financials must be understood within the broader AI industry’s capital cycle.

Amazon, Google, and others invest in Anthropic, sell it compute, distribute Claude through cloud marketplaces, and compete elsewhere in AI.

These relationships overlap the roles of partner, investor, supplier, and competitor. Reuters states that approximately 47% of Anthropic’s 2025 revenue came through Amazon and Google cloud marketplaces, while two undisclosed customers each accounted for approximately 12% of revenue.

*Source label: Investing.com.*

The 47% figure is a distribution-channel share. It cannot be read as cloud providers themselves buying 47% of Claude services.

If channels gain bargaining power, a model company’s technical advantage may not fully translate into profit. Conversely, if Anthropic has become an important customer and revenue source for cloud platforms, it may negotiate better procurement terms.

In industrial-economics terms, the question is: who has more bargaining power in the AI value chain—the provider of scarce computing resources, or the model and application platforms creating end demand?

## VI. The Hacker News Community: Five Economic Questions Behind the Disagreements

The supplied material records approximately 199 comments in the specified Hacker News discussion at the time it was captured, with views still changing quickly. This is not a scientific sample and cannot establish the attitude of the entire developer community, but it can reveal real technical and investment disagreements.

*Source label: Hacker News.*

### Debate One: Is $500B a Debt Cliff or Normal Long-Term Investment?

User trjordan argues that everything ultimately comes back to whether real demand can persist. If demand continues to surge, enormous capacity contracts can support revenue at scale. If demand breaks, however, the impact will be severe.

Another user, Maxatar, points out that many comments mistake hundreds of billions of dollars in cumulative ten-year commitments for annual costs, exaggerating short-term operating pressure.

*Source label: Hacker News.*

These views are compatible.

Contractual commitments are obligations across periods, rather than debt falling due entirely in the current year. But if demand drops abruptly in a future year, fixed payments can still cause cash flow to deteriorate quickly.

A year-by-year payment ladder is needed, beyond the aggregate commitment figure.

### Debate Two: Will Open-Source Models Eventually Push Inference Prices Close to Cost?

Community user jacquesm argues that competition from Chinese AI models and open-weight models may make today’s leading-model prices unsustainable. Other users emphasize that Claude still has clear quality and usability advantages in actual coding tasks.

*Source label: Hacker News.*

This points to two competing equilibria:

| Future market state | Basis for Anthropic’s profitability |
|---|---|
| Frontier models retain a substantial performance gap | Performance premiums, brand, and service reliability |
| Multiple models approach similar capabilities | Cloud channels, workflows, and cost efficiency |
| Open-source models satisfy most needs sufficiently | High-end tasks, proprietary tools, and enterprise services |
| Much inference moves to local or private clusters | Software platforms, orchestration, security, management, and specialized models |

Lower open-weight model costs do not necessarily eliminate Anthropic’s value.

They may, however, shift the source of that value from the model itself toward enterprise tools, ecosystem integration, and highly reliable complete services.

This is why “technical leadership” and a “sustainable economic moat” must be studied separately.

### Debate Three: Do Attractive Gross Margins Mean the Company Is Already Profitable?

Some HN commenters are optimistic about the improvement in revenue relative to compute expenses. Others point out that model training, R&D, stock-based compensation, and continuing infrastructure investment must still be borne by shareholders.

Some commenters also mistake noncash fair-value losses on financing instruments for direct cash outflows, prompting debate.

*Source label: Hacker News.*

My analytical framework distinguishes four layers:

| Metric | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Inference contribution margin | Is each incremental unit of usage profitable? |
| Standard GAAP gross margin | What are direct delivery costs under applicable accounting rules? |
| Operating margin | Are R&D, sales, administration, and other operations profitable overall? |
| Free cash flow and returns after equity dilution | Can shareholders ultimately earn sustainable economic returns? |

The easily overlooked issue is that foundation models require continual retraining to remain competitive. Even with high inference contribution margins, R&D costs cannot permanently be treated as one-off expenses.

### Debate Four: Does AI Expand Its Market or Replace Its Own Customers?

One user argues that even if all professional developers subscribed to expensive AI coding services, the resulting revenue might still struggle to support a market capitalization of several trillion dollars. If AI substantially reduces developer headcount, the seat-based market could instead shrink.

The opposing view is that AI expands the market for automatable tasks, making market estimates based only on the existing developer population too conservative.

*Source label: Hacker News.*

I believe the correct economic unit should shift from developer headcount to work completed.

Suppose a company employs 100 developers, each paying $200 a month for an AI subscription, for total spending of $20,000.

If it later needs only 30 developers but AI agents perform much of the execution, testing, operations, and maintenance previously required, the company may still be willing to pay far more than $20,000 a month for AI.

AI would then be priced by delivered workload, process-cost savings, or business outcomes, rather than employee numbers.

Whether platforms can actually achieve that pricing power depends on their ability to verify task quality and prevent enterprises from switching to cheaper alternatives.

### Debate Five: Can an IPO Transfer AI Technology Risks to Ordinary Investors?

Early and related HN discussions of Anthropic’s IPO repeatedly raise the possibility that risks borne by private investors may shift to public shareholders, pension funds, and index investors after listing. Some commenters also argue that even genuinely valuable AI technology can receive an excessive public-market valuation.

*Source label: Hacker News.*

This is a familiar financial-market phenomenon: the eventual success of a technological revolution does not guarantee positive returns for investors who buy technology companies at peak valuations.

The internet did transform the global economy, but some companies bought at high valuations in 2000 still inflicted permanent capital losses on investors.

Assessing Anthropic likewise requires moving beyond “will AI change the world?” to how much future cash flow the current share price already anticipates.

## VII. Corporate Governance: An Institutional Experiment in AI Safety and Control over Capital

Anthropic’s governance is more complex than an ordinary technology company’s dual-class share structure.

### 7.1 Three Layers of Governance

| Governance layer | Arrangement described in the supplied material | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Founder LLC / Class F | 50.1% | Cofounder voting control over general shareholder matters |
| Long-Term Benefit Trust | Majority of board seats | Independent trustee mechanism emphasizing long-term public benefit and the safety mission |
| Public Benefit Corporation | Delaware public benefit corporation | Within applicable law, the board balances shareholders’ financial interests, specified public-benefit goals, and the interests of those materially affected by corporate conduct |

Reporting on public documents says the seven cofounders will collectively control 50.1% of voting power on most general voting matters through a specific equity structure, while the independent Long-Term Benefit Trust retains the right to select a majority of directors. Ordinary Class A shareholders may therefore have limited influence.

*Source label: Reuters.*

This creates a distinctive principal–agent problem.

Public shareholders supply capital and bear economic risk without necessarily receiving control commensurate with their economic interests. The founding team holds important voting control at the operating level, alongside an independent safety-centered governance structure.

From a corporate-governance perspective, this may reduce management short-termism, but may also make external oversight harder, create conflicts over board authority, and make capital-allocation mistakes harder to correct.

### 7.2 Can the Safety Mission Conflict with Shareholder Returns?

Anthropic acknowledges in public material that its public-benefit mission may lead to decisions inconsistent with shareholders’ financial interests.

*Source label: Reuters.*

In the short term, refusing commercially attractive but higher-risk model deployments can reduce revenue.

Over the long term, strict safety governance could also become a commercial advantage if it reduces enterprise customers’ legal risk and increases procurement confidence among governments and highly regulated industries.

A PBC structure therefore does not inevitably damage shareholder value. Its economic effects should be assessed through observable outcomes such as safety-incident frequency, enterprise retention, policy penalties, lost commercial opportunities, and the long-term cost of capital.

In October 2026, Anthropic also announced plans to allocate some equity to a new Public Benefit Fund, initially 5%, with a mechanism for possible increases as company value grows. Some matters still require shareholder approval.

*Source label: Anthropic.*

The announced value of a public-benefit commitment, its eventual legal force, and actual asset transfers must be distinguished.

## VIII. Valuation Analysis: What Does $1.5T–$2T Mean?

Public documents have not yet confirmed the offering price or final post-offering share count, so a definitive IPO market capitalization cannot be calculated.

The following uses the $1.5T and $2T figures in market reporting as valuation assumptions, rather than official Anthropic offering guidance. Reuters reported on October 26 that some investors’ expected valuation had shifted from nearly $2T toward approximately $1.5T.

*Source label: Reuters.*

### 8.1 Price-to-Sales Analysis

Compare two revenue bases:

- Annualized first-half 2026 revenue: $16.29B × 2 = $32.58B.
- Annualized 2026 Q3 revenue: $16.8B × 4 = $67.2B.

The latter is a run rate closer to the current business scale. It is not realized full-year revenue and does not guarantee that the next four quarters can sustain that level.

### Implied P/S at Different Valuations

| Annualized revenue base | Implied P/S at $1.5T | Implied P/S at $2.0T |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2026 H1 annualized: $32.58B | Approximately 46.0x | Approximately 61.4x |
| 2026 Q3 annualized: $67.2B | Approximately 22.3x | Approximately 29.8x |

These multiples divide the valuation assumptions in the supplied material by the respective annualized revenues. They are not confirmed offering valuations.

Viewed solely through P/S, the pricing depends substantially on future growth being delivered.

High-growth companies cannot, however, be valued by mechanically applying mature software companies’ historical sales multiples. The variables that determine a reasonable valuation are future cash-flow conversion, long-term margins, the duration of growth, equity dilution, and the cost of capital.

### 8.2 A 2030 Scenario Valuation Model

To quantify these variables, consider a simplified terminal-value model of equity free cash flow.

Assumptions:

- The valuation reference date is around year-end 2026.
- The forecast endpoint is year-end 2030, four years later.
- The equity discount rate is 12%, solely an illustrative assumption.
- 2030 free cash flow uses different revenue and margin scenarios.
- A terminal cash-flow multiple estimates 2030 equity value.
- Interim cash flows, additional financing, equity dilution, and changes in net cash or debt are excluded for now.

### Anthropic 2030 Valuation Sensitivity

All scenarios are model assumptions, not management forecasts or market consensus.

| Assumption | Conservative | Base | Optimistic |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2030 revenue | $100B | $200B | $350B |
| FCF margin | 15% | 25% | 30% |
| 2030 FCF | $15B | $50B | $105B |
| Terminal multiple | 25x | 30x | 35x |
| Discounted 2026 value | $0.24T | $0.95T | $2.34T |

The calculation is 2030 FCF × terminal multiple ÷ 1.12⁴. This is not a full DCF model, especially because it does not yet address how hundreds of billions of dollars in compute contracts affect cash flow and equity-financing needs.

The table carries the values in the original scenario-valuation chart. $1.5T and $2.0T are comparison assumptions only, not established IPO prices.

The three scenarios illustrate how sensitive valuation is to revenue growth and free-cash-flow margins, rather than predicting a precise price.

In this model, if the market ultimately values Anthropic at $1.5T–$2T, investors effectively need to believe that the company can achieve long-term revenue and free cash flow close to the optimistic scenario.

### 8.3 Reverse Valuation: What Is Needed to Justify $1.5T?

Assume that in 2030:

- The free-cash-flow margin reaches 30%.
- Terminal equity value is 30x free cash flow.
- The equity discount rate remains 12%.

Then:

| Current valuation assumption | Required 2030 revenue | CAGR from the Q3 annualized base |
|---|---:|---:|
| $1.5T | Approximately $262B | Approximately 40.6% |
| $2.0T | Approximately $350B | Approximately 51.0% |

These figures use the original draft’s four-year horizon, 12% discount rate, 30% FCF margin, and 30x terminal multiple, retaining its approximate values.

The key to the current valuation therefore goes beyond whether revenue can set another record over the next year. It is whether the company can sustain approximately 40%–50% annualized revenue growth over several years while achieving a high free-cash-flow margin.

This is a demanding combination of assumptions. With competitors continuing to cut API prices and the company continuing to sign long-term compute contracts, revenue growth and high margins may not be achievable simultaneously.

## IX. Current Developments Affecting the IPO

### 9.2 Chinese Open Models and the Model-Distillation Dispute

The report said these customers had contributed approximately 5%–7% of revenue in the first nine months of 2026. The characterization of their behavior is Anthropic’s claim.

*Source label: Reuters.*

From a competitive-strategy perspective, this reveals three issues.

First, closed APIs cannot fully ensure exclusive control of foundation-model knowledge and capabilities.

Second, model providers compete over the protectability of their capabilities as well as price, including anti-distillation measures, access controls, model-weight security, and inference-side behavior monitoring.

Third, protecting a moat can conflict with near-term commercial growth. Blocking potentially abusive traffic can reduce capability leakage while affecting revenue and service availability.

This tension interacts with Anthropic’s public-benefit governance structure.

### 9.3 Tension Between AGI Safety Commitments and the Technology Race

The S-1 risk disclosures cover potential deception, resistance to shutdown, and information manipulation shown by models in controlled tests. Reuters particularly highlights the relationship between these risk disclosures and the competitive pressure on Anthropic to keep releasing new models.

*Source label: Reuters.*

The distinction must be clear: problems observed in controlled safety evaluations are not confirmed production incidents.

For investors, potential consequences could nevertheless include reputational damage, regulatory liability, litigation costs, delayed model releases, and restrictions on some business activities.

Unlike traditional software companies, frontier-model companies face uncertainty from both product markets and the potential impact of changes in model capabilities on legal and social institutions.

### 9.4 On-Chain Pre-IPO Trading May Aid Price Discovery but Is Not Formal Equity Ownership

## X. 2026–2030: Which AI Startup Opportunities Merit Attention?

The most important implication of Anthropic’s prospectus for AI founders is what it reveals about the industry’s cost constraints, distribution structure, and value allocation.

I focus on the following five directions.

### 01. AI Compute Economics / FinOps

Multimodel cost accounting, token-cost attribution, GPU utilization analysis, workload forecasting, provider-procurement decisions, and inference-cost budgeting.

Potential customers are medium-to-large enterprises that use multiple models and cloud platforms over the long term. The value rests on verifiable compute-cost savings rather than the model’s own performance lead.

The main challenge is that cloud-native tools may erode the market for independent providers.

### 02. Model-Agnostic Agent Runtime

Build model-independent agent execution platforms covering workflow orchestration, state persistence, permission controls, auditing, task recovery, and cross-model routing.

If foundation models become commoditized, enterprises will place greater value on freedom to change models and continuity of business processes.

The real moat is reliable execution of critical workflows, beyond simple API aggregation.

### 03. AI Agent Evaluation & Governance

Provide agent observability, online evaluation, anomalous-behavior detection, tool-permission controls, sensitive-action approvals, and chains of audit evidence.

The more capable frontier models become at executing valuable tasks, the more enterprises need to demonstrate controlled behavior, trustworthy results, and compliance with internal policies.

Commercial value can be verified through fewer safety incidents, lower audit costs, and faster deployment.

### 04. Vertical Outcome-Based Agents

Industry agents priced by specific business outcomes, such as financial reconciliation, customer-service case handling, cross-border ecommerce product operations, compliance-document review, and software quality assurance.

This model can partly avoid price competition based only on tokens or subscription seats, tying the commercial relationship to the customer’s actual economic benefit.

Key risks concern deployment complexity, exception handling, and attribution of business outcomes.

### 05. Enterprise Model Portability

Help enterprises migrate among Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, open-weight models, and private deployments, building systems for capability testing, cost comparison, and compliant delivery.

The stronger model competition becomes, the greater the potential value of reducing vendor lock-in.

This direction requires assessment of real enterprise workloads, beyond comparing public benchmark rankings.

These opportunities share a common feature: they do not depend on any one foundation model remaining permanently ahead.

More importantly, they can keep delivering value as model performance improves, prices fall, providers change, and even technical architectures shift.

All five directions nevertheless face the risk of direct integration by major cloud platforms and model providers. Startups therefore need real customer relationships, business-data integration, industry workflows, or specialized evaluation capabilities, rather than relying only on generic SDK wrappers.

## XII. Final Research Conclusions

Based on the supplied material’s cross-analysis of key prospectus disclosures, contemporaneous capital-market reporting, and Hacker News debates, three assessments are most worth retaining.

First, Anthropic has demonstrated that foundation models can generate very large-scale real commercial demand, particularly through enterprise APIs, coding tools, and automated workflows. The marked improvement in first-half 2026 operating margins is a more important fundamental signal than the annualized revenue run rate.

Second, substantial uncertainty remains around Anthropic’s long-term shareholder returns. Capital-expenditure commitments, training costs, cloud-channel bargaining power, model commoditization, governance, and future dilution can all affect eventual free cash flow. Rapid revenue growth alone therefore cannot establish an adequate margin of safety at a $1.5T–$2T valuation.

Third, Anthropic’s IPO is a public-market test of the entire foundation-model industry’s economic structure. Once the company lists, investors will be able to observe its return on capital, operating cash flow, customer expansion, and cost changes more frequently. These data will also serve as an important reference for assessing other unlisted frontier-model companies.

One central hypothesis worth further testing is:

> AI capabilities can keep advancing without foundation-model company margins necessarily rising alongside them. The socioeconomic value created by AI may ultimately far exceed the shareholder economic value that foundation-model providers can retain.

This resembles some historical patterns in infrastructure revolutions such as railways, electricity, aviation, telecommunications, and the internet. Technological diffusion can create enormous social welfare while intensifying producer competition, leaving users, downstream application companies, and owners of complementary assets to capture part of the economic gains.

From this perspective, Anthropic’s prospectus is both an IPO disclosure and important material for studying capital accumulation, technological progress, and the allocation of economic rents in the AI era.

The issues truly worth following extend beyond whether Anthropic can become a company worth several trillion dollars: at what cost structure will AI intelligence be supplied, who will bear long-term investment risks, and who will ultimately control pricing against customers’ willingness to pay?
